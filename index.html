[01] Calculator

Your level

Work through the six steps. The result updates as you go.

Step 1 Headphone sensitivity

Find this on the headphone's spec sheet or box. It is written as dB/mW (dB SPL per milliwatt) or dB/V (dB SPL per volt). Pick the unit your spec sheet uses.

Unit
Step 2 Headphone impedance

Also on the spec sheet, in ohms (Ω). Typical values run from 16 Ω to 300 Ω.

Step 3 Source output at full volume

How much signal your phone, computer or amp can deliver at 100% volume. Spec sheets give it either as volts RMS, or as power in mW into a load such as "30 mW into 32 Ω". Pick whichever form you have. If you can't find it, 1 V is a reasonable starting guess for a phone or laptop.

Step 4 Volume slider type

Choose the device or system whose volume slider you use. Each one turns slider position into loudness differently. Linear is the naive straight-line model, included for comparison.

Step 5 Volume position

Set the slider to where you normally listen. The presets are shortcuts.

Step 6 What you play optional

The calculator assumes a full-scale tone, which is the loudest possible signal. Music is quieter on average, so choose a lower option for a more realistic figure.

[04] Method

The maths

  1. Slider to gain. The curve turns the slider position into an attenuation in dB, and the gain is 10^(dB ÷ 20).
  2. Voltage. Volts at the headphone = max output voltage × gain.
  3. Power. P = V² ÷ Z, shown in mW.
  4. Level. SPL = sensitivity (dB/mW) + 10·log10(P in mW) + content offset. A dB/V rating is converted first: dB/mW = dB/V − 10·log10(1000 ÷ Z).
  5. Safe time. NIOSH allows 85 dBA for 8 hours and halves the time for every 3 dB above: T = 8 h ÷ 2^((L − 85) ÷ 3).
  6. Dose. Each listening session adds its minutes divided by the safe time at its level. 100% is the daily allowance.

Limits of this estimate

  • It uses spec-sheet figures. It is a model, not a measurement. Only a calibrated meter or ear coupler measures the real level.
  • The Windows, Android and Apple curves are approximations. Drivers, manufacturers and Bluetooth codecs change the real gain.
  • Bluetooth headphones and many wireless dongles apply their own gain stage on top of the OS slider, which this does not model.
  • The levels are unweighted. NIOSH limits are in dBA, so the result is conservative for music with heavy bass.
  • Ear coupling shifts the level by a few dB. Open-back versus closed-back and the IEM tip seal both matter.
  • Limits describe population averages. This is not medical advice. Ringing or muffled hearing after listening means turn it down and rest your ears.
